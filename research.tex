\title{A Two-Graph Model of Fleet Coordination\\[3pt]
\large Physical Feasibility, Competitive Dispatch, and Fleet-Profit Optimization}
\author{}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We formulate coordination of a distributed battery fleet using two coupled
graphs. A radial distribution graph $G_1$ constrains simultaneous injections;
each battery's time-expanded state-of-charge graph $G_2^i$ describes its
admissible intertemporal trajectories. The coupling is through joint network
constraints and an aggregate-dependent electricity price. We distinguish
threshold heuristics, independent price-taking optimization, competitive
equilibria, finite-player Nash equilibria, and centralized fleet-profit
optimization. Under convex trajectory constraints, a competitive variational
equilibrium maximizes a concave integral potential. For affine price impact,
the finite-player game also has an exact potential, different from both the
competitive potential and fleet profit. We prove existence and qualified
uniqueness results, central dominance over any feasible benchmark, a
separability result identifying when coordination has zero value, and an
additive bound on the profit gain attributable to affine price impact. An
explicit counterexample shows that no universal $4/3$ bound applies to the
profit ratio. We distinguish fleet value from system welfare and retail
portfolio value. The four-coincident-peak (4CP) transmission-cost regime, and
its scheduled replacement, are used as institutional context for the strategy
graph rather than as an independent revenue claim; we show that for a
residential fleet coincident-peak timing is monetized through the energy and
portfolio channels already modeled, not through a transmission credit.
Previously reported numerical results are retained with their provenance and
interpretation limits; the mathematical claims do not depend on reproducing
those simulations.
\end{abstract}

\section{Scope, Information, and Notation}
\label{sec:setup}

The primary question is how much additional operating value a common owner
can obtain by jointly scheduling batteries, relative to an explicitly
specified independent controller. The two-graph construction separates
\emph{electrical feasibility} from \emph{intertemporal choice and economic
interaction}. A positive premium is an outcome to measure, not an assumption.

Fix a finite horizon of dispatch intervals
$\T=\{0,\ldots,T-1\}$ with duration $\Delta>0$ hours. State variables are
indexed by $t=0,\ldots,T$. Unless otherwise stated, all comparisons are
deterministic, conditional on the same exogenous data and information.
An optimization using realized future data is a perfect-foresight benchmark;
it is not a causal operating policy. A stochastic or rolling-horizon extension
must impose the same information filtration on every controller being compared.

\section{Graph 1: Physical Distribution Feasibility}
\label{sec:g1}

\subsection{Radial network and linearized flow}
Let $G_1=(\mathcal N_1,\mathcal E_1)$ be a rooted radial feeder with root $0$.
A fleet spanning several feeders can be represented by a forest, with an
independent root condition for each component. A single feeder is sufficient
for the definitions below. Each battery is assigned to a specified node;
several batteries may share a node.

For a directed edge $e=(m,n)$ away from the root, write
$\operatorname{down}(n)$ for the downstream node set. In a lossless
LinDistFlow approximation, real-power flow is
\begin{equation}
 P_{et}=\sum_{k\in\operatorname{down}(n)}d_{kt}
       -\sum_{i:\,n(i)\in\operatorname{down}(n)}q_{it}.
 \label{eq:flow}
\end{equation}
Let $R_{et}$ denote reactive-power flow in MVAr, avoiding confusion with fleet
injection $Q_t$. In the base model, reactive demands and $R_{et}$ are fixed,
and batteries inject or withdraw only real power. Let $r_e,x_e$ be
per-unit impedances on a common three-phase base $S_{\rm base}$ MVA, and
$v_{nt}=|V_{nt}|^2$ the per-unit squared voltage. Then
\begin{equation}
 v_{nt}=v_{0t}-\frac{2}{S_{\rm base}}
 \sum_{e\in\mathcal P(0\to n)}(r_eP_{et}+x_eR_{et}).
 \label{eq:voltage}
\end{equation}
The powers and impedances must be converted consistently if another unit
system or single-phase convention is used.

\subsection{Thermal and voltage constraints}
For a specified apparent-power rating $\overline S_e$, impose
\begin{align}
 P_{et}^2+R_{et}^2&\le\overline S_e^2,
 &&e\in\mathcal E_1,\ t\in\T, \label{eq:thermal}\\
 \underline V_n^2\le v_{nt}&\le\overline V_n^2,
 &&n\in\mathcal N_1,\ t\in\T. \label{eq:voltagebounds}
\end{align}
When $R_{et}$ is fixed and $|R_{et}|\le\overline S_e$, define
$K_{et}=\sqrt{\overline S_e^2-R_{et}^2}$. Equation~\eqref{eq:thermal}
then becomes the linear constraint $-K_{et}\le P_{et}\le K_{et}$.
If $|R_{et}|>\overline S_e$, the prescribed operating point is infeasible.
If reactive control is instead optimized, the thermal condition is a convex
second-order cone constraint and inverter capability limits must also be added.

\begin{definition}[Joint instantaneous feasibility]
Let $\mathcal A_t\subseteq\R^N$ be the set of battery injections satisfying
\eqref{eq:flow}--\eqref{eq:voltagebounds}, together with any specified
connection import/export limits. In the fixed-reactive model,
$\mathcal A_t$ is a closed convex polyhedron.
\end{definition}

These constraints certify feasibility only within the stated balanced,
linearized electrical model. They do not certify a full unbalanced AC power
flow, protection coordination, or transient response. Local voltage and
frequency measurements alone cannot establish every upstream thermal limit.
Network parameters and aggregate downstream loading must be supplied or
estimated to evaluate $\mathcal A_t$.

\section{Graph 2: Battery Trajectories and Joint Constraints}
\label{sec:g2}

\subsection{Local dynamics and boundary conditions}
The local battery model is
\begin{align}
 e_{i,t+1}&=e_{it}+\Delta\left(\eta_{c,i}c_{it}
                        -\frac{g_{it}}{\eta_{d,i}}\right),
 &&t\in\T, \label{eq:soc}\\
 0\le c_{it}&\le\overline P_i^c,\qquad
 0\le g_{it}\le\overline P_i^d, &&t\in\T, \label{eq:powerboxes}\\
 \underline e_{it}&\le e_{it}\le E_i, &&t=0,\ldots,T, \label{eq:energybounds}\\
 e_{i0}&=e_i^{\rm init},\qquad e_{iT}=e_i^{\rm init}.
 \label{eq:boundary}
\end{align}
Here $\underline e_{it}$ is a declared household backup reserve. The terminal
equality gives an energy-neutral accounting horizon and prevents apparent
profits from unpriced depletion of initial energy. A different terminal set
or terminal value is permissible, but must be the same in every benchmark and
included in the objective where appropriate. Time-dependent availability and
power bounds can be added as local constraints.

The exact operating model additionally imposes
\begin{equation}
 c_{it}g_{it}=0,\qquad i=1,\ldots,N,\ t\in\T.
 \label{eq:exclusive}
\end{equation}
Let $\X_i^{\rm ex}$ denote this generally nonconvex local set. For convex
analysis, use the explicit relaxation $\X_i^{\rm rel}$ obtained by replacing
\eqref{eq:exclusive} with
\begin{equation}
 \frac{c_{it}}{\overline P_i^c}
 +\frac{g_{it}}{\overline P_i^d}\le1,
 \label{eq:moderelax}
\end{equation}
assuming positive ratings; unavailable actions are set to zero separately.
This is the convex hull of the two instantaneous charging/discharging line
segments. It is not asserted to be the convex hull of the complete
intertemporal exact set.

\begin{remark}[When the relaxation certifies an executable optimum]
A relaxed optimum satisfying \eqref{eq:exclusive} is also a global optimum
of the exact problem with the same objective, since
$\X_i^{\rm ex}\subseteq\X_i^{\rm rel}$. Without this check, the relaxed
central value is an upper bound. Negative prices, terminal conditions, and
network constraints can make simultaneous charging and discharging attractive
in a relaxation. It must not be assumed absent without proof or verification.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Time-expanded graph representation}
For each battery, choose finite energy bins and a finite action menu.
$G_2^i$ has nodes $(t,e)$ and directed edges
$(t,e)\to(t+1,e')$ whenever a menu action $(c,g)$ satisfies
\eqref{eq:soc} and the local bounds. Fix the initial node and permitted
terminal nodes according to \eqref{eq:boundary}. A path specifies a battery
trajectory. At fixed prices, the profit attached to an edge is
\begin{equation}
 \Delta\big[p_t(g-c)-\kappa_i(c+g)\big].
 \label{eq:edgeprofit}
\end{equation}
Consequently, local price-taking optimization on this finite acyclic graph
is a longest-path problem, or a shortest-path problem after negating profits.

One path per battery is an indivisible, discrete choice. Fractional path flows
represent a relaxation or a divisible population of batteries; they do not
automatically represent the exact operation of one physical battery. Convex
theorems below apply to the declared continuous relaxation, not automatically
to this discrete path-selection problem. A discretized exact path problem is
a finite restriction of the continuous exact problem; no discretization-error
bound is claimed here.

\subsection{Coupling the two graphs}
For $s\in\{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{rel}\}$, define
\begin{equation}
 \X^s=\left\{x\in\prod_i\X_i^s:
                   (q_{1t},\ldots,q_{Nt})\in\mathcal A_t
                   \text{ for every }t\in\T\right\}.
 \label{eq:jointset}
\end{equation}
In the fixed-reactive model, write these shared constraints compactly as
$Ax\le b$. Each block $A_i$ maps battery $i$'s trajectory into the shared
network inequalities, so $Ax=\sum_iA_ix_i$.

$G_1$ excludes \emph{joint combinations} of transitions in the $G_2^i$.
An individual edge cannot generally be declared network-feasible without the
other batteries' simultaneous actions. The joint strategy graph can be
viewed as a product graph with inadmissible combinations removed; explicit
construction of that exponentially large graph is unnecessary for continuous
trajectory optimization.

\begin{assumption}[Feasibility for the mathematical results]
\label{ass:feasible}
The horizon and all power/energy bounds are finite, and the chosen joint
feasible set is nonempty. The convex results use
$\X=\X^{\rm rel}$, a compact convex set. Statements using the exact or finite
path model name that model explicitly. Network constraints and household
reserves are identical across the regimes being compared.
\end{assumption}

\section{Price Model, Payoffs, and Distinct Benchmarks}
\label{sec:regimes}

\subsection{Aggregate price impact}
For each interval, assume
\begin{equation}
 p_t(Q_t)=f_t(L_t-Q_t),
 \label{eq:price}
\end{equation}
where $f_t$ is continuous and nondecreasing on an interval containing $L_t$
and all feasible net loads. This includes the reference point needed for
integrals starting at zero injection. Use differentiability only where
derivatives are stated.
This is a reduced-form market-impact model, not a complete ERCOT clearing
model. Supply availability, renewable output, fuel costs, reserves, and
congestion may vary with $t$ and with the conditioning scenario. A common
single price is an explicit simplifying assumption.

The affine specialization is
\begin{equation}
 p_t(Q_t)=a_t-\beta_tQ_t,\qquad\beta_t\ge0,
 \label{eq:affine}
\end{equation}
with $\beta_t$ measured in $(\$/\mathrm{MWh})/\mathrm{MW}$.
At a fixed price vector $p$, battery $i$'s price-taking objective is
\begin{equation}
 U_i(x_i;p)=\sum_t\Delta p_tq_{it}-D_i(x_i).
 \label{eq:fixedpayoff}
\end{equation}
Its realized strategic payoff, when its action affects price, is instead
\begin{equation}
 \pi_i(x)=\sum_t\Delta q_{it}p_t(Q_t)-D_i(x_i).
 \label{eq:strategicpayoff}
\end{equation}

The affine scalar model excludes cross-location and cross-time price effects.
With a vector price $p(q)$, a competitive integral potential requires suitable
integrability conditions (in particular a symmetric Jacobian on a simply
connected differentiable domain); it does not follow from aggregation alone.

\subsection{Threshold and independent-optimization benchmarks}
A threshold policy is a specified map from available information and local
state into charging, discharging, or idling, for example using thresholds
$\ell_{it}(e)<u_{it}(e)$. Its forecast, thresholds, power sizing, reserve
handling, and terminal treatment are part of its definition. Following a
shared price signal does not itself make such a policy a Nash equilibrium.

An independent horizon optimizer instead selects
\begin{equation}
 \widehat x_i\in\argmax_{x_i\in\X_i}U_i(x_i;\widehat p)
 \label{eq:independent}
\end{equation}
against a supplied exogenous forecast $\widehat p$. Generally
$\widehat p\ne p(Q(\widehat x))$; thus independent optimization need not be
a competitive equilibrium either.

Independent local trajectories can violate shared feeder limits. One explicit
offline benchmark with network protection is
\begin{equation}
 x^{\rm B}=\argmin_{x\in\X}
       \frac12(x-\widehat x)^\top W(x-\widehat x),\qquad W\succ0,
 \label{eq:projection}
\end{equation}
where the positive-definite, dimensionally scaled weights are fixed in advance.
On the convex set this projection exists and is unique. It includes network,
reserve, and terminal conditions and optimizes proximity to requested
trajectories, not profit. It is a shared safety intervention; the benchmark
must be described as independent scheduling with network protection, not as
fully autonomous operation. An online alternative must respect the same
information available to the central controller and update SoC from executed
actions. No claim is made that all safety interventions yield the same value.

\section{Competitive Equilibrium and the Integral Potential}
\label{sec:competitive}

\subsection{Product constraints: a price-consistent competitive solution}
First remove the shared feeder constraints and let $\X=\prod_i\X_i$.
A price-taking competitive solution is a profile $x^{\rm W}$ such that
\begin{equation}
 x_i^{\rm W}\in\argmax_{x_i\in\X_i}
       U_i\big(x_i;p(Q(x^{\rm W}))\big)
 \quad\text{for every }i.
 \label{eq:competitivefixedpoint}
\end{equation}
Each battery treats the price as fixed when deviating; the prices are then
required to be consistent with aggregate action. This is the competitive
price-taking condition, or its nonatomic/Wardrop interpretation. For a finite
fleet, it is not the Nash condition for the strategic payoff
\eqref{eq:strategicpayoff}.

\subsection{Shared constraints: a variational selection}
Let $H_i$ be the linear map $x_i\mapsto q_i$. Define
\begin{equation}
 F_i^{\rm W}(x)=\nabla D_i(x_i)-\Delta H_i^\top p(Q(x)).
 \label{eq:competitivefield}
\end{equation}

\begin{definition}[Competitive variational equilibrium]
On the common convex feasible set $\X$, a competitive variational equilibrium
is $x^{\rm W}\in\X$ satisfying
\begin{equation}
 \sum_i\left\langle F_i^{\rm W}(x^{\rm W}),y_i-x_i^{\rm W}\right\rangle
 \ge0\qquad\text{for all }y\in\X.
 \label{eq:competitivevi}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

This definition imposes a particular treatment of shared constraints.
Where Lagrange multipliers exist, a common network multiplier $\lambda\ge0$
supports individual optimization of
$U_i(x_i;p)-\lambda^\top A_ix_i$, with
$\lambda^\top(Ax-b)=0$. Standard relative-interior constraint qualifications
are sufficient for this multiplier representation. Independent controllers
responding only to the public energy price need not implement it.

\begin{proposition}[Correct potential and aggregate uniqueness]
\label{prop:potential}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:feasible}, define
\begin{equation}
 \Phi(x)=\sum_t\Delta\int_0^{Q_t(x)}p_t(s)\,\dd s
                  -\sum_iD_i(x_i).
 \label{eq:potential}
\end{equation}
The function $\Phi$ is concave. Its global maximizers exist and coincide
with the solutions of \eqref{eq:competitivevi}. On a product feasible set,
these are exactly the competitive solutions
\eqref{eq:competitivefixedpoint}. If each $p_t$ is strictly decreasing on
the feasible aggregate interval, all maximizers have the same aggregate
injection vector $Q^{\rm W}$. Individual trajectories need not be unique.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The derivative of $\int_0^{Q_t}p_t(s)\,\dd s$ is $p_t(Q_t)$, a
nonincreasing function, so the integral is concave. Since $Q$ is linear and
$D_i$ is convex, $\Phi$ is concave on $\X$; continuity and compactness give
existence. Its block gradient is
$\nabla_i\Phi=\Delta H_i^\top p(Q)-\nabla D_i=-F_i^{\rm W}$.
The first-order condition for a differentiable concave maximum on a convex
set is therefore exactly \eqref{eq:competitivevi}. On a product set this
condition is equivalent to the separate first-order conditions for each
concave fixed-price problem. If two maximizers had different aggregate
vectors, strict concavity of the integral in $Q$, combined with convexity of
the feasible set and costs, would make their midpoint strictly better, a
contradiction.
\end{proof}

If $f_t$ is differentiable, the second derivative of the integral with
respect to $Q_t$ is $-f_t'(L_t-Q_t)\le0$. Thus the convention in
\eqref{eq:potential} requires \emph{maximization}; a cost-minimization
convention must use $-\Phi$. The potential depends on full trajectories when
costs differ across batteries; aggregate $Q$ alone need not determine cost.
Strict aggregate uniqueness also does not imply uniqueness of a nonconvex
exact or discrete-path equilibrium.

\section{Finite-Player Nash Equilibrium: An Exact Affine Potential}
\label{sec:nash}

For product strategy sets, a Nash equilibrium is a profile at which no
battery can increase \eqref{eq:strategicpayoff} through a unilateral local
deviation. With shared constraints, define
$\X_i(x_{-i})=\{y_i:(y_i,x_{-i})\in\X\}$ and use feasible unilateral
deviations in this set; the resulting concept is a generalized Nash
equilibrium (GNE).

\begin{proposition}[Exact potential for affine impact]
\label{prop:atomic}
Under \eqref{eq:affine}, the strategic game has exact payoff potential
\begin{equation}
 \Psi(x)=\sum_t\Delta\left[
       a_tQ_t-\frac{\beta_t}{2}Q_t^2
              -\frac{\beta_t}{2}\sum_iq_{it}^2\right]
              -\sum_iD_i(x_i).
 \label{eq:atomicpotential}
\end{equation}
Every global maximizer of $\Psi$ on a nonempty compact feasible set is a
GNE, and is an ordinary Nash equilibrium if the feasible set is a product.
On a convex product set, Nash equilibria are exactly the global maximizers.
On a shared convex set, global maximizers are exactly the
\emph{variational} Nash equilibria; other GNE need not maximize $\Psi$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Fix $x_{-i}$. Write $Q_t=q_{it}+Q_{-i,t}$ and expand the terms of
\eqref{eq:atomicpotential} that depend on $x_i$. They are
\[
 \sum_t\Delta\big[a_tq_{it}-\beta_tq_{it}Q_{-i,t}
                         -\beta_tq_{it}^2\big]-D_i(x_i),
\]
which equal $\pi_i(x_i,x_{-i})$. All other terms are constant in $x_i$.
Hence any unilateral payoff difference equals the corresponding potential
difference, establishing the exact-potential identity. A global maximizer
therefore admits no profitable feasible unilateral deviation. Continuity and
compactness ensure that at least one such maximizer exists.
For convex sets, $\Psi$ is concave because $\beta_t\ge0$, and its block
gradient is the player's own-payoff gradient. On a product set, the separate
Nash first-order conditions combine into the global first-order condition
for $\Psi$; concavity makes it sufficient. With shared constraints, the
same global condition is precisely the variational Nash condition.
\end{proof}

The exact-potential identity also applies to a finite path-selection game
with affine impact. It guarantees existence of a pure equilibrium via a
global potential maximizer, but not that every pure equilibrium globally
maximizes the potential. For general nonlinear impact, the affine identity
does not extend without additional structure.

This model need not be a classical unweighted Rosenthal congestion game:
injections are signed and players can contribute different magnitudes to a
time slot. Proposition~\ref{prop:atomic} proves the needed affine result
directly. In a classical Rosenthal cost game, a pure Nash equilibrium is a
profile with no unilateral potential-decreasing deviation; every global
potential minimum is such an equilibrium. These minima need not minimize
total social cost \cite{rosenthal}.

\section{Centralized Fleet Dispatch and Its Mathematical Guarantees}
\label{sec:central}

\subsection{Objective and existence}
Define realized fleet operating profit by
\begin{equation}
 \Pi(x)=\sum_i\pi_i(x)
       =\sum_t\Delta Q_t p_t(Q_t)-D(x),\qquad D=\sum_iD_i.
 \label{eq:fleetprofit}
\end{equation}
A centralized fleet optimum is
\begin{equation}
 x^{\cen}\in\argmax_{x\in\X}\Pi(x).
 \label{eq:central}
\end{equation}
Continuity and compactness ensure existence, including for the exact
nonconvex model. They do not by themselves give an efficient global solver.

In the affine case,
\begin{equation}
 \Pi(x)=\sum_t\Delta(a_tQ_t-\beta_tQ_t^2)-D(x).
 \label{eq:centralqp}
\end{equation}
This is concave. On $\X^{\rm rel}$ with fixed reactive flow, the base model
is a concave quadratic maximization over linear constraints, equivalently a
convex quadratic minimization. If $\beta_t>0$ at every interval, the optimal
aggregate $Q^{\cen}$ is unique, by the midpoint argument in
Proposition~\ref{prop:potential}. This need not determine a unique dispatch
allocation across batteries. With charging/discharging binaries, it becomes
a mixed-integer convex quadratic formulation, whose global optimum must be
certified separately.

For twice differentiable $f_t$, the revenue term
$h_t(Q)=Qf_t(L_t-Q)$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
 h_t''(Q)=-2f_t'(L_t-Q)+Qf_t''(L_t-Q).
 \label{eq:curvature}
\end{equation}
Thus $Qf_t''(L_t-Q)\le2f_t'(L_t-Q)$ on the feasible range is a
sufficient intervalwise condition for concavity. Convexity of $f_t$ alone
does not suffice. Piecewise curves and price caps require a separate
concavity assessment; a local optimum is not automatically global.

\subsection{The marginal incentives are different}
Before efficiencies, degradation, and constraint multipliers are included,
the price-related marginal terms are
\begin{equation}
 \begin{array}{ll}
 \text{competitive price taker:} & p_t(Q_t),\\[2pt]
 \text{finite strategic battery }i: & p_t(Q_t)+q_{it}p_t'(Q_t),\\[2pt]
 \text{central fleet:} & p_t(Q_t)+Q_tp_t'(Q_t).
 \end{array}
 \label{eq:marginals}
\end{equation}
For affine impact, these become $p_t$, $p_t-\beta_tq_{it}$, and
$p_t-\beta_tQ_t$. These are marginal \emph{price components}, not complete
optimality conditions. For example, with marginal stored-energy value
$\lambda_{i,t+1}$ and no binding inequality constraints, stationarity for a
positive discharge and a positive charge, respectively, has the form
\[
 m_{it}-\kappa_i-\lambda_{i,t+1}/\eta_{d,i}=0,
 \qquad
 -m_{it}-\kappa_i+\eta_{c,i}\lambda_{i,t+1}=0,
\]
where $m_{it}$ is the applicable marginal term, before network adjustments.
These equations refer to the relevant active operating mode. Binding
constraints replace equalities with the appropriate KKT inequalities.
There is generally no single constant threshold for the entire fleet, and
no theorem that central dispatch is smaller in every high-price interval.

\subsection{Zero coordination value under separability}
\begin{proposition}[Separability benchmark]
\label{prop:separable}
If prices are exogenous, the feasible set is a product, and the objective
is additive over batteries, then
\begin{equation}
 \max_{x\in\prod_i\X_i}\sum_iU_i(x_i;p)
       =\sum_i\max_{x_i\in\X_i}U_i(x_i;p).
 \label{eq:separable}
\end{equation}
Thus centralized optimization cannot improve on independent optimal
dispatch under the same information and local constraints.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Every summand is bounded above by its individual maximum. Selecting an
individual maximizer for each battery attains the sum, since their product
is feasible. Compactness guarantees these maximizers exist.
\end{proof}

This result does not require convexity. Shared physical constraints or
endogenous prices destroy the stated separability. They permit, but do not
guarantee, a positive premium. Beating a threshold heuristic when the problem
is separable establishes the value of better local scheduling, not a need
for joint control.

\section{Coordination Value: Valid Bounds and a Counterexample}
\label{sec:premium}

\subsection{Common scoring and central dominance}
Let $x^{\rm B}\in\X$ be a specified feasible baseline: a protected threshold
policy, protected independent optimizer, competitive variational equilibrium,
or Nash equilibrium. Evaluate both regimes using the same exogenous scenario,
price-response function, constraints, and accounting objective, but their
\emph{own induced prices}. Write
\begin{equation}
 V_{\cen}=\Pi(x^{\cen}),\quad V_{\rm B}=\Pi(x^{\rm B}),\quad
 \Delta V_{\rm B}=V_{\cen}-V_{\rm B},\quad
 \rho_{\rm B}=\frac{V_{\cen}}{V_{\rm B}}\quad(V_{\rm B}>0).
 \label{eq:premiumdef}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Dominance and certified comparisons]
\label{prop:dominance}
If $x^{\cen}$ is a global optimum and $x^{\rm B}$ is feasible for the same
problem, then $\Delta V_{\rm B}\ge0$. If also $V_{\rm B}>0$, then
$\rho_{\rm B}\ge1$. A feasible computed central schedule with value
$\underline V_{\cen}$ and a valid global upper bound $\overline V_{\cen}$
give
\[
 \underline V_{\cen}-V_{\rm B}\le\Delta V_{\rm B}
                    \le\overline V_{\cen}-V_{\rm B}.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The baseline is an admissible candidate in the central maximization. The
remaining statements follow by subtraction and, for the ratio, division
by a positive number.
\end{proof}

A relaxed solution that violates exact operating constraints is not a
feasible lower bound for the exact central problem. A rolling-horizon
controller also need not dominate another controller on every realized
trajectory. Report $\Delta V_{\rm B}$ and dollars per battery alongside the
ratio; omit the ratio when the baseline is nonpositive or too close to zero
to be informative. Fixed ownership costs cancel in $\Delta V$ but can
substantially alter a profit ratio, so the accounting scope must be stated.

\subsection{A valid additive bound for affine impact}
\begin{proposition}[Market-impact premium bound]
\label{prop:impactbound}
Under affine impact, let $x^{\rm W}$ globally maximize the competitive
potential and $x^{\cen}$ globally maximize fleet profit on the same set.
Define
\begin{equation}
 K(x)=\frac{\Delta}{2}\sum_t\beta_tQ_t(x)^2.
 \label{eq:impactpenalty}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
 0\le\Pi(x^{\cen})-\Pi(x^{\rm W})
 \le K(x^{\rm W})-K(x^{\cen})
 \le K(x^{\rm W}).
 \label{eq:impactbound}
\end{equation}
In particular $K(x^{\cen})\le K(x^{\rm W})$. If $\Pi(x^{\rm W})\ge v_0>0$
and $|Q_t|\le\overline Q_t$, then
\begin{equation}
 1\le\rho_{\rm W}
 \le1+\frac{\Delta\sum_t\beta_t\overline Q_t^2}{2v_0}.
 \label{eq:conditionalratio}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For affine impact, $\Phi(x)=\sum_t\Delta(a_tQ_t-\beta_tQ_t^2/2)-D(x)$,
so $\Pi=\Phi-K$. Global potential optimality gives
$\Phi(x^{\cen})-\Phi(x^{\rm W})\le0$. Subtracting the corresponding
$K$ terms yields the middle bound; central optimality gives the lower
bound. Since $K\ge0$, the remaining inequalities follow.
\end{proof}

On the convex model, Proposition~\ref{prop:potential} identifies the
potential maximizer with the competitive variational equilibrium. The
bound does not apply to an arbitrary threshold baseline or an arbitrary GNE.
It establishes a small \emph{absolute} impact premium when impact slopes
and aggregate dispatch are small. A near-unity ratio additionally requires
a baseline profit bounded away from zero. With $\beta_t=0$ for all $t$,
competitive variational and central fleet optimization have the same
objective even if shared network constraints bind. Implementing that
variational equilibrium already incorporates network coordination.

\subsection{No universal affine profit-ratio bound}
\begin{proposition}[Unbounded ratio in an affine two-period example]
\label{prop:counterexample}
There is no universal upper bound of $4/3$, or any other finite constant,
on $\rho_{\rm W}$ for the battery-profit objective under affine prices.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Consider two one-hour intervals, lossless batteries, no degradation, no
binding feeder constraints, zero initial and terminal energy, and aggregate
cycling capacity $C<50$ MWh with matching power capacity. Each battery can
charge in period 1 and discharge in period 2. Let $z\in[0,C]$ be aggregate
energy shifted, so $Q_1=-z$ MW and $Q_2=z$ MW. Set
\[
 p_1=20+z,\qquad p_2=120-z.
\]
The competitive price spread is $100-2z>0$ for every $z\le C$, so each
price-taking battery uses its full capacity: $z^{\rm W}=C$. Fleet profit is
$\Pi(z)=100z-2z^2$. For $25\le C<50$, the centralized optimum is
$z^{\cen}=25$, with profit $1250$. Hence
\[
 \rho_{\rm W}(C)=\frac{1250}{100C-2C^2}\longrightarrow\infty
 \qquad\text{as }C\uparrow50.
\]
Every denominator in this limit is strictly positive, and all prices are
affine. This proves the claim.
\end{proof}

For $C=49$, the arithmetic is:

\toprule
Regime & Shifted MWh & Charge \$/MWh & Discharge \$/MWh & Profit (\$)\\
\midrule
Competitive & 49 & 69 & 71 & 98\\
Central fleet & 25 & 45 & 95 & 1,250\\

The same capacity-saturated profile is also a finite-player Nash equilibrium
for $N\ge49$ equally sized batteries in this $C=49$ example. At the proposed
profile, battery $i$'s own-profit derivative with respect to its shifted
energy is $100-2(49)-2(49/N)=2-98/N\ge0$. Its payoff is concave in that
energy, so its constrained optimum is its upper bound $49/N$.

The classical $4/3$ theorem concerns the ratio of total routing costs for
fixed-demand nonatomic routing with nonnegative affine latency functions
\cite{roughgarden}. It is not invariant under a change from total cost to
arbitrage profit. No scarcity nonlinearity is required for the counterexample.

\section{Fleet Profit, System Welfare, and Retail Portfolio Value}
\label{sec:welfare}

\subsection{A system-cost interpretation requires an additional assumption}
Suppose, specifically for a welfare model, that $f_t(\ell)=C_t'(\ell)$ is
the genuine marginal system production cost for exogenous demand $\ell$,
with no omitted network costs or externalities. Then
\begin{equation}
 C_t(L_t)-C_t(L_t-Q_t)=\int_0^{Q_t}f_t(L_t-s)\,\dd s.
 \label{eq:welfare}
\end{equation}
Consequently $\Phi(x)$ is avoided production cost minus degradation, and
its maximizer minimizes total system production cost plus degradation on
the same feasible set. Under these assumptions, competitive dispatch can be
system-efficient even when it earns the fleet less than \eqref{eq:central}.

Market settlement prices, scarcity adders, and offers need not equal true
marginal social costs. Without the additional assumption above, this welfare
interpretation is not justified. Throughout this paper, \eqref{eq:central}
is called \emph{fleet-profit optimal}, not a system social optimum. A profit
gain from altering prices is not by itself evidence of a system-efficiency
gain or a conclusion about market-rule compliance.

\subsection{Retail exposure changes the owner's objective}
For a simplified single-price retail portfolio, let $B_t$ be residual
unhedged household demand before controlled battery dispatch, with positive
$B_t$ denoting net purchases. Assume fixed retail receipts, fixed hedge
payments, and settlement of all residual energy at $p_t(Q_t)$. The
dispatch-dependent portfolio margin is
\begin{equation}
 \Pi^{\rm REP}(x)=\sum_t\Delta(Q_t-B_t)p_t(Q_t)-D(x)
                    +\text{constants}.
 \label{eq:rep}
\end{equation}
Relative to zero dispatch, its incremental value is
\begin{equation}
 V^{\rm REP}(x)=\sum_t\Delta\left[(Q_t-B_t)p_t(Q_t)+B_tp_t(0)\right]-D(x).
 \label{eq:repvalue}
\end{equation}
This reference is an accounting comparison; operational dominance over zero
dispatch requires that zero dispatch actually be feasible.
For affine impact, the marginal price component is
\begin{equation}
 p_t(Q_t)-\beta_t(Q_t-B_t).
 \label{eq:repmarginal}
\end{equation}
Lower prices benefit the owner's remaining net purchases. Therefore
$B_t$ can materially change optimal dispatch. Locational basis, actual hedge
settlements, retail tariffs, losses, and other charges require a richer
portfolio model. The battery-only objective is recovered by setting $B_t=0$.
The affine bound in Proposition~\ref{prop:impactbound} is stated only for
the battery-only objective, not automatically for retail portfolio value.

Base's published customer documentation states that it owns and schedules
its batteries \cite{base}. Accordingly, independent controllers are a
counterfactual operating architecture, not a claim that homeowners actually
play a strategic dispatch game.

\section{Coincident-Peak Timing: Regulatory Context and a Prediction--Deployment Problem}
\label{sec:cp}

The four-coincident-peak (4CP) transmission-cost regime is the institutional
reason a single 15-minute interval can carry outsized economic weight in
ERCOT. We use it in two ways: as \emph{context} that motivates treating the
coincident-peak (CP) interval as a distinguished target on $G_2$, and as a
\emph{stochastic extension} of the strategy graph. We deliberately do not
treat 4CP avoidance as an independent revenue term for a residential fleet;
the subsection on exposure explains why, and shows that CP-timing value, where
it exists, is already carried by the objectives \eqref{eq:fleetprofit} and
\eqref{eq:repvalue}.

\subsection{The 4CP mechanism and its scheduled replacement}
Under 4CP, ERCOT allocates transmission cost of service to distribution
providers according to each provider's load during the single highest
15-minute system-wide demand interval in each of June, July, August, and
September; the following calendar year's transmission charge scales with that
coincident-peak demand \cite{ercot4cp,rtoinsider}. An illustrative
postage-stamp rate on the order of \$68.55/kW-year has been referenced in the
current proceeding, so a demand-metered load that removes consumption from all
four peaks can avoid transmission charges on the order of tens of thousands of
dollars per MW-year \cite{ercot4cp,nrg}. This regime is scheduled to change:
under Texas Senate Bill 6, the Public Utility Commission of Texas must overhaul
the methodology by December 31, 2026, with a twelve-coincident-peak (12CP)
approach measured in 30-minute intervals under active consideration in Project
No.~58484 \cite{sb6reform,rtoinsider}. We therefore parameterize the setting by
a target-interval count $m$ and an interval length $\Delta_{\rm CP}$, rather
than fixing $m=4$ and $\Delta_{\rm CP}=15$ minutes; every statement below is
stated in those parameters.

\subsection{Who is exposed: an attribution the model must respect}
4CP is a billing determinant only for interval/demand-metered accounts, in
practice larger commercial and industrial loads. Residential and
small-commercial transmission cost is recovered through volumetric
(\$/kWh) or fixed allocations that are not tied to the coincident-peak
intervals; there is accordingly no direct per-meter transmission saving from
residential curtailment during a CP, and any class-level effect is socialized
across the rate class rather than captured by an individual participant
\cite{nrg,huntsman4cp}. Two consequences are load-bearing for this paper.

\begin{remark}[CP timing is an energy-price object, not a transmission credit, for a residential fleet]
\label{rem:attribution}
For a fleet behind residential meters, coincident-peak timing is monetized, if
at all, through the energy and ancillary channels already modeled: the
fleet-profit objective \eqref{eq:fleetprofit} for an arbitrage-only owner and
the retail-portfolio objective \eqref{eq:repvalue} for a load-serving owner
that avoids wholesale purchases at the peak. It is not an additional
transmission-charge term. Reporting a coincident-peak strategy as ``4CP
savings'' for such a fleet would double count or misattribute value that is
in fact temporal energy-price arbitrage.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Cycling does not reduce metered energy]
\label{rem:roundtrip}
Storage cycling does not reduce total metered consumption. With round-trip
efficiency $\eta_{c,i}\eta_{d,i}<1$, serving load from the battery during a CP
and refilling later strictly increases total grid import over the cycle by the
conversion loss. A flat volumetric transmission charge is therefore not
lowered, and is marginally raised, by CP time-shifting. The recoverable value
is entirely the temporal spread in the energy price (and any ancillary or
program payment), captured by \eqref{eq:repvalue}: purchase in low-price
intervals is displaced from purchase in high-price intervals, not eliminated.
\end{remark}

A further modeling point: with high solar penetration the gross-load CP
interval (afternoon) and the scarcity price interval (net-load evening)
increasingly diverge, and CP intervals can occur when prices are comparatively
low \cite{nrg}. A residential fleet should target the interval that carries the
monetizable energy/ancillary spread, which need not be the gross-load CP that
sets transmission allocation.

\subsection{The coincident-peak interval as an uncertain target}
Within a CP window, let the realized peak interval $\tau$ be a random element
of a candidate set $\mathcal C\subseteq\T$ with distribution $\pi$ induced by
weather, demand, renewable output, and---endogenously---by the aggregate
curtailment of all CP-responsive load. Aggregate avoidance flattens and can
displace the peak; this signal degradation is documented and is part of the
regulatory case for reform \cite{nrg,rtoinsider}. A fleet that is a negligible
share of system load---on the order of tens of MW against a summer peak near
$95$ GW \cite{peak2026}---does not materially move $\pi$ and best-responds to
it. This is the taker (nonatomic) posture already used in
Section~\ref{sec:limit}, applied now to the distribution over the peak's
\emph{timing} rather than its price level.

\subsection{Deployment as optimal stopping under a shared budget}
Over the window, several candidate high-load intervals are revealed
sequentially with forecasts. The fleet holds a limited, costly discharge
capability: cycle wear, energy conversion loss, and household backup reserve
$\underline e_{it}$ that is most valuable precisely on the hottest,
highest-outage-risk days. Choosing when to commit the fleet against the running
maximum is an optimal-stopping (prophet-type) problem; committing on a false
alarm consumes budget and backup that may be needed for the true peak. This is
the intertemporal counterpart, under timing uncertainty, of the deterministic
dispatch in Sections~\ref{sec:central}--\ref{sec:premium}.

\subsection{When does coordinating the deployment add value?}
The separability logic of Proposition~\ref{prop:separable} transfers directly.

\begin{proposition}[Zero deployment-coordination premium under separability]
\label{prop:cpseparable}
If the timing distribution $\pi$ is exogenous, each battery has its own
budget, the feasible set is a product, and the objective is additive over
batteries and risk-neutral, then the fleet-optimal expected deployment equals
the sum of independently optimal per-battery deployments, and centralized
deployment adds no expected value.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Under the stated hypotheses the expected objective is a sum of per-battery
functionals over per-battery feasible sets, so the maximization separates
exactly as in Proposition~\ref{prop:separable}; the expectation over $\pi$,
common to all batteries, does not couple them.
\end{proof}

A positive deployment-coordination premium therefore requires at least one
coupling channel, exactly paralleling the deterministic case:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Shared network constraints.} A feeder or transformer that cannot
pass the simultaneous discharge of all co-located batteries makes $\mathcal
A_t$ bind within a group, so the fleet must allocate scarce feeder headroom
across batteries and intervals. This is a $G_1$ effect.
\item \textbf{A pooled budget.} A fleet-level cycle, wear, or backup allowance,
rather than a per-battery one, must be allocated across heterogeneous
batteries; the coordinator spends the cheapest available headroom first and
spares backup-critical homes.
\item \textbf{Risk aversion.} If the owner is averse to the lumpy realized
payment or charge, hedging deployment across several candidate intervals
reduces variance; expected value alone does not capture this.
\end{enumerate}
A distinct centralized-forecasting gain---pooling fleet telemetry to sharpen
$\pi$---is a prediction improvement, not deployment coordination, and should be
accounted separately so the two are not conflated.

\begin{remark}[Crowding raises the hedging value]
As aggregate CP avoidance intensifies, $\pi$ becomes more diffuse, which
raises the value of hedged deployment relative to point-forecast firing: the
deployment-coordination premium of channel~3 is increasing in forecast
dispersion. This does not raise total available value, which the same
flattening reduces.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Placement in the two-graph model}
The CP setting adds a stochastic weight to $G_2$: a trajectory that is armed
and available at the realized $\tau$ carries an option value proportional to
the monetizable peak spread in \eqref{eq:repvalue}, while $G_1$ supplies the
shared constraint of channel~1 that can make coordinated deployment strictly
better than independent deployment. As everywhere in this paper, the premium is
a consequence of shared constraints, endogenous prices, or risk, not of
synchronization as such. Operationally, per-customer (micro) prediction
supplies each battery's availability and cost inputs, and the fleet (macro)
layer solves the stochastic, feeder-constrained, budget-limited deployment
against $\pi$.

\section{Large-Fleet Scaling Without an Unsupported Limit Claim}
\label{sec:limit}

Consider a sequence of fleets indexed by $N$, and write
$q_{it}^{(N)}=a_{it}^{(N)}/N$. Then
\begin{equation}
 Q_t^{(N)}=\sum_iq_{it}^{(N)}
           =\frac1N\sum_i a_{it}^{(N)}.
 \label{eq:scaling}
\end{equation}
If the empirical distribution of the rescaled actions converges weakly to
$\mu_t$ and these actions are uniformly bounded, then
$Q_t^{(N)}\to\int a\,\mu_t(\dd a)$. By contrast,
$N^{-1}\sum_iq_{it}^{(N)}=Q_t^{(N)}/N\to0$ under bounded aggregate power.
Convergence of the empirical distribution is an assumption here, not a
consequence of the fleet's size alone.

For affine impact the finite-player and competitive potentials satisfy
\begin{equation}
 \Psi(x)=\Phi(x)-\frac{\Delta}{2}\sum_t\beta_t\sum_iq_{it}^2.
 \label{eq:limitpotential}
\end{equation}
If $|q_{it}|\le\varepsilon_N\to0$ and
$\sum_i|q_{it}|\le M_t$ uniformly, then
\begin{equation}
 0\le\Phi(x)-\Psi(x)
 \le\frac{\Delta\varepsilon_N}{2}\sum_t\beta_tM_t\longrightarrow0.
 \label{eq:uniformbound}
\end{equation}
The bound follows from $q_{it}^2\le\varepsilon_N|q_{it}|$. It shows why
individual price-impact corrections vanish while the fleet correction
$\beta_tQ_t$ can remain finite. It does not, without further assumptions
on convergence of feasible sets and equilibrium selections, prove convergence
of the equilibrium profiles.

Common price signals or common shocks can preserve correlated behavior.
Conditional laws of large numbers can remove idiosyncratic noise while the
aggregate remains random through common noise. Thus no deterministic
mean-field limit, mean-field-game existence result, or diversification of
common signals is assumed solely from $N\to\infty$.

\subsection{Accounting optimism versus recoverable coordination value}
For affine impact anchored at $p_t(0)=a_t$, valuing a \emph{fixed schedule}
at no-fleet prices gives
\begin{align}
 \Pi^{\rm naive}(x)&=\sum_t\Delta a_tQ_t-D(x),\\
 \Pi^{\rm naive}(x)-\Pi(x)&=\Delta\sum_t\beta_tQ_t^2=2K(x).
 \label{eq:naivegap}
\end{align}
This identity measures accounting optimism from ignoring market impact.
It does not equal the recoverable gain from changing dispatch. In
particular, Proposition~\ref{prop:impactbound} bounds the competitive-to-central
gain by $K(x^{\rm W})$, at most half the naive-accounting gap for that
competitive schedule in this affine model. The original study's reported
approximately \$500,000 naive versus \$323,000 realized value (about
$1.55\times$), and its $1.15\times$ figure for 50,000 units, should be
classified as reported accounting-impact comparisons, not coordination
premiums. Equation~\eqref{eq:naivegap} applies only if those runs actually
use the affine price model and the same fixed schedule on both sides.

\subsection{Minimal checks for the existing implementation}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Declare the feasible model.} State whether paths are indivisible,
charge/discharge exclusivity is exact, or a convex relaxation is used.
Verify SoC recursions, reserve and terminal conditions, and every feeder limit
on executed actions. For a relaxed solution, inspect $c_{it}g_{it}$.
\item \textbf{Identify the baseline.} Distinguish threshold output,
independent horizon optimization, competitive potential optimization, and
finite-player potential optimization. Record any safety projection.
\item \textbf{Use an equilibrium certificate when claiming equilibrium.}
On the convex model, a global first-order gap is
\[
 r_{\rm W}(x)=\max_{y\in\X}\langle\nabla\Phi(x),y-x\rangle\ge0.
\]
It is zero exactly at a competitive variational equilibrium. Replace
$\Phi$ with $\Psi$ for a variational finite-player Nash certificate. A
general GNE instead requires checking feasible unilateral improvement
$\max_{y_i\in\X_i(x_{-i})}[\pi_i(y_i,x_{-i})-\pi_i(x)]$ for every $i$;
these tests certify different equilibrium concepts.
\item \textbf{Certify the central solve.} For concave $\Pi$, the analogous
first-order gap bounds the objective suboptimality. For an exact mixed-integer
model, retain the incumbent value and global bound. Use tolerances and data
precision small relative to the claimed dollar premium.
\item \textbf{Check the zero-value case.} With $\beta_t=0$ and nonbinding
shared constraints, independent optimal and central values must agree
within numerical accuracy by Proposition~\ref{prop:separable}.
\item \textbf{Separate mechanisms.} Evaluate a two-by-two design: binding
versus nonbinding network constraints, crossed with zero versus positive
price impact. Compare threshold and independent optimal controls separately.
Network and price effects can interact; their gains are not presumed additive.
\item \textbf{Use comparable information and scoring.} Apply the same
forecasts, scenario, reserve policy, terminal treatment, and degradation
costs. Score each dispatch at its own induced prices. State the horizon and
report $\Delta V$, dollars per battery, and a ratio only when meaningful.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{ERCOT anchoring and identification limits}
ERCOT publishes SCED locational marginal prices normally every five minutes
and settlement-point prices every 15 minutes \cite{ercot-lmp,ercot-spp}.
Historical hub and load-zone settlement prices are publicly cataloged
\cite{ercot-history}. Dispatch resolution and settlement aggregation must
be matched explicitly; a hub average is a proxy, not automatically the
fleet's actual settlement exposure. A residential fleet settling at a load
zone should use that zone's price, not a hub average.

One transparent counterfactual specification is
\begin{equation}
 p_t(Q_t)=p_t^{\rm obs}-\beta_t(Q_t-Q_t^{\rm ref}).
 \label{eq:historicalanchor}
\end{equation}
The reference dispatch $Q_t^{\rm ref}$ must match the price anchor. Setting
it to zero treats the modeled fleet as an incremental hypothetical fleet;
it is not a reconstruction of an existing fleet whose actions already
affected observed prices. Historical price/load correlation alone does not
identify the causal slope $\beta_t$: supply, renewable output, congestion,
and reserve conditions also change. Until a credible counterfactual market
model or identification strategy is available, report impact slopes as
scenario assumptions and sweep them, including zero.

Market vintage must also be explicit. ERCOT implemented Real-Time
Co-optimization Plus Batteries on December 5, 2025 \cite{ercot-rtcb}.
The legacy ORDC price-adder framework was replaced by ancillary-service
demand curves with that transition \cite{ercot-ordc}. A legacy ORDC
specification should therefore be tied to an appropriate historical period,
not silently applied as the current market-clearing mechanism. A fixed
offer-cap truncation is an additional modeling assumption, not a general
derivation of settlement prices or price-impact slopes.

\section{What the Two-Graph Model Establishes}
The physical graph supplies shared feasibility constraints; the strategy
graphs supply local intertemporal choices. Their combination gives a
well-defined dispatch problem without identifying synchronization with
equilibrium or assuming that centralization must create substantial value.
Competitive, finite strategic, and fleet-optimal dispatch have different
objectives. Their relationship can be proved under stated convexity and
price assumptions, and evaluated against the same physical constraints.

The most direct empirical case for centralized control is a measured
improvement over a declared feasible independent baseline when feeder limits
or other shared constraints matter. Market-impact value is a distinct,
assumption-sensitive component. Coincident-peak timing, driven by the
4CP regime and its scheduled 12CP successor, sharpens \emph{when} that value
concentrates but, for a residential fleet, is realized through the energy and
portfolio objectives rather than a transmission credit. The retained
simulations can support claims about their own scenarios after verification,
while the propositions above specify which conclusions hold beyond those
scenarios.

\appendix
\section{Optional Allocation and Participation Accounting}
\label{app:allocation}

Allocation is separate from dispatch optimality. For a realized nonnegative
gain $\Delta V$, choose declared weights $\phi_i\ge0$ with $\sum_i\phi_i=1$
and set $M_i=\phi_i\Delta V$. This rule is budget-balanced and nonnegative;
it does not establish marginal contribution, incentive compatibility,
individual rationality, or a scarcity-targeting rule. A Shapley allocation
requires a separately defined coalition-value game and is not automatically
nonnegative without further assumptions on that game.

If one \emph{postulates} the scalar utility
\begin{equation}
 R_i(\theta)=M_i(2\theta-\theta^2)-h_i\theta^2,
 \qquad0\le\theta\le1,\quad M_i,h_i\ge0,
 \label{eq:participation}
\end{equation}
then, for $M_i+h_i>0$, strict concavity gives
\begin{equation}
 \theta_i^*=\frac{M_i}{M_i+h_i},\qquad
 R_i(\theta_i^*)=\frac{M_i^2}{M_i+h_i}.
 \label{eq:theta}
\end{equation}
If $M_i=h_i=0$, every $\theta\in[0,1]$ is optimal. These formulas follow
from the assumed utility, not from the two-graph game; no dispatch rule is
derived from them. In particular, battery-specific interpolation between
two feasible fleet schedules can violate joint network constraints. A
\emph{common} interpolation coefficient preserves feasibility only in a
convex feasible model, and need not preserve exact operating modes. This
optional participation model is not used in any main theorem or reported
simulation comparison.
