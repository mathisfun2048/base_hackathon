\begin{center}
\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=gray!5,colframe=gray!55,width=0.97\linewidth,boxrule=0.5pt,arc=2pt]
\small A distributed battery fleet is valuable because it can act as a single
coordinated market participant. This review asks the mirror question: \emph{if a
competitor built the same infrastructure and pointed that coordination the wrong
way, how much could they bend the market and the grid?} We answer it from first
principles --- the same equations that measure the fleet's \emph{value} measure an
adversary's \emph{ceiling} --- validate on real ERCOT data, and pair every risk
with the control that bounds it. Everything runs on public or synthetic models;
no real circuit is targeted.
\end{tcolorbox}
\end{center}

% =====================================================================
\qsec{The whole argument in one idea}

A battery fleet has power over the price and the grid only when it is a
\textbf{big enough, coordinated} participant during the \textbf{few hours the grid
is short}. The lever it pulls is simple: \textbf{withhold} --- hold energy back
(or, worse, charge up) exactly when everyone else needs power. That single action
does two things at once, and this is the unifying thesis of the whole review:

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[x=1cm,y=1cm,>={Stealth[length=6pt]},font=\small]
  \node[draw,fill=basered!12,rounded corners,minimum width=3.4cm,minimum height=0.9cm,
        align=center] (act) at (0,0) {\textbf{Withhold during scarcity}\\(the market-making move)};
  \node[draw,fill=baseorange!12,rounded corners,minimum width=3.8cm,align=center]
        (mkt) at (5.7,1.15) {\textbf{Price spikes} $\;\Rightarrow\;$ every MWh in\\Texas pays it: a wealth transfer};
  \node[draw,fill=baseblue!10,rounded corners,minimum width=3.8cm,align=center]
        (phys) at (5.7,-1.15) {\textbf{Reserves vanish} $\;\Rightarrow\;$ the grid\\tips toward rolling blackouts};
  \draw[->,thick,basered] (act.east) -- (mkt.west);
  \draw[->,thick,basered] (act.east) -- (phys.west);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\noindent The same withheld megawatt is both a price signal and a missing reserve.
So ``manipulate the price'' and ``destroy the grid'' are not two attacks --- they
are one action seen two ways. The catch is \textbf{scale}: it only works if the
fleet is large. That is the reason this is a forward-looking review and not a
historical one.

\begin{crit}
Today's fleet is ${\sim}20{,}000$ units (${\sim}400$ MW, $0.4\%$ of the grid) and
cannot tip a healthy system. But the fleet \textbf{triples every year}: at that
rate it reaches ${\sim}240{,}000$ units in ${\sim}2$ years --- past the
${\sim}200{,}000$ needed to tip an already-stressed grid. \textbf{The
infrastructure to do this is being built now, on a two-year clock.}
\end{crit}

% =====================================================================
\act{Act I}{The math: where leverage comes from}

Everything rests on three equations from the technical report. When a battery
decides how much to inject, the price it should reckon with depends on how much of
its own effect on the market it internalizes. There are exactly three cases:
\[
\begin{array}{lll}
\textbf{Price-taker (competitive):} & p_t(Q_t) & \text{ignores its own impact}\\[3pt]
\textbf{Semi (one strategic unit $i$):} & p_t(Q_t)+q_{it}\,p_t'(Q_t) & \text{internalizes its \emph{own} slice}\\[3pt]
\textbf{Big market-maker (whole fleet):} & p_t(Q_t)+Q_t\,p_t'(Q_t) & \text{internalizes the \emph{entire} fleet}
\end{array}
\]
For a locally linear price ($p_t=a_t-\beta_tQ_t$) these read $p_t$, $p_t-\beta_t
q_{it}$, and $p_t-\beta_t Q_t$. The three differ by a single term --- how much
price impact is folded in --- and that term is the whole story:

\begin{itemize}
\item The price-taker leaves money on the table (ignores $p'$).
\item The big market-maker internalizes the fleet's \emph{total} impact
$Q_t\,p_t'$. To keep the price high it \textbf{withholds}. That is market power,
and it is exactly the ``self-cannibalization'' a value-seeking owner \emph{avoids}
by not dumping all its energy at once.
\item \textbf{Below a scale threshold the problem is separable}: coordinating many
small units buys \emph{nothing} ($\beta_t\!\to\!0$ or no shared limit $\Rightarrow$
the joint optimum is just the sum of independent ones). Leverage --- for the owner
\emph{and} for an adversary --- switches on only past that threshold.
\end{itemize}

% =====================================================================
\act{Act II}{The flip: owner value $=$ adversary capability}

Keep the same two graphs (the physical feeder $G_1$ and each battery's
state-of-charge graph $G_2$); flip only the objective. The number that measures how
much an owner \emph{gains} by coordinating --- the coordination premium $\rho$ ---
is, sign for sign, the number that measures how much extra \emph{harm} an adversary
can do by coordinating. The correlation the owner diversifies \emph{away} is exactly
what the adversary drives \emph{up}. This is why one analysis serves both: the
fleet's value and its attack ceiling are the same couplings viewed with opposite
intent.

\begin{adv}
Leverage over price and grid $=$ endogenous price ($\beta$) $+$ shared network
limits ($G_1$) $+$ a detection budget, and it \textbf{switches on at a scale
threshold}. A competitor with the same infrastructure inherits the same lever ---
they just point it the wrong way.
\end{adv}

% =====================================================================
\act{Act III}{ERCOT reality: the lever, priced in dollars}

Is $\beta$ --- the price impact --- actually big enough to matter? On real ERCOT
Load-Zone data it is \emph{tiny in normal hours and steep only in scarcity}: the
local sensitivity is about \textbf{$3{,}100\times$ larger} during scarcity
($\beta\!\approx\!0.79$ vs.\ $2.5\times10^{-4}$ \$/MWh per MW). Scarcity is the only
window where the lever exists --- which is also exactly where the grid is fragile.

The reason a small fleet can still move a lot of money is structural: in a
single-clearing-price market, \textbf{every megawatt-hour in Texas settles at the
one marginal price}. A few dollars per MWh, multiplied by the entire state's load,
is enormous --- and it is a \emph{transfer} from ratepayers, not value created.

\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.99\linewidth]{results/figures/O10_market_power.png}
\end{center}

\begin{crit}
A hostile fleet during one 2-hour scarcity window pushes what all of Texas pays
\textbf{up toward the \$5{,}000 cap} as the fleet grows. Even a \emph{legal,
profit-seeking} owner is negative-sum: it earns roughly \textbf{\$1 for every
\$61} it costs everyone else, because the price is set at the margin but paid on
all load.
\end{crit}

And the physical face of the same lever --- withholding reserves --- is bounded by
how stressed the grid already is. On today's healthy grid it takes ${\sim}195{,}000$
coordinated units (${\sim}10\times$ the fleet) to force rolling blackouts:
impossible. But on an already-critical grid (ERCOT hit EEA2 on 2023-09-06, reserves
below $1{,}750$ MW), only ${\sim}8{,}000$ units --- $40\%$ of today's fleet ---
would be the marginal push.

\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.99\linewidth]{results/figures/O7_system_crash.png}
\end{center}

\begin{crit}
The fleet becomes a system-level trigger only once reserves are already near empty
(below ${\sim}2{,}230$ MW). It cannot cold-start a blackout --- it can only
\textbf{amplify an already-critical interval}. But at $240{,}000$ units that
amplification is enough on its own.
\end{crit}

\begin{means}
The single highest-value control: \textbf{forbid coordinated \emph{charging} /
withholding during grid emergencies} (low reserves). During scarcity the fleet must
support the grid, never draw from it or hold back. This removes the physical face of
the lever entirely.
\end{means}

% =====================================================================
\act{Act IV}{Topology: the wiring changes the game}

The three equations hide an assumption: that $\beta$ is one number. It is not.
An injection moves the scarcity price only as much as the network lets it
\emph{reach} the choke point, so $\beta$ becomes $\beta\cdot a_n$, where $a_n$ is a
node's \textbf{price leverage} --- computed from the grid's shift factors (standard
power-flow linear algebra). The three equations become
\[
p,\qquad p-\beta\,a_n\,q_{it},\qquad p-\beta\,a_n\,Q_t,
\]
and the topology decides how much havoc the market-maker term ($p-\beta a_n Q$) can
do. The adversary's optimal play falls straight out: \textbf{withhold where leverage
is high ($a_n$ large), sell where it is low.}

\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.99\linewidth]{results/figures/O12_topology_game_theory.png}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Single-gateway grids} (one line in, hub-and-spoke): every node's
leverage \emph{aligns}, so coordinating the fleet is ${\sim}4\times$ more powerful
than a lone unit --- the worst case.
\item \textbf{Meshed grids} (many paths): an injection splits across paths, so
leverage is \emph{diluted} (${\sim}3\times$).
\item \textbf{Congested export} (a long, limited line): capacity stuck behind the
congestion has \emph{zero} price leverage --- it is stranded. Topology decides which
of the fleet's megawatts even \emph{have} market power.
\end{itemize}

\begin{crit}
Same fleet, same three equations --- but the market-making harm ranges from
\textbf{\$290M to \$359M} in one window purely by \emph{where on the grid} the fleet
sits. Whether a competitor can wreck the grid is a property of the \textbf{wiring},
not just the fleet's size.
\end{crit}

\begin{means}
The defense follows the same math: a \textbf{per-node market-share screen keyed to
$a_n$} --- watch hardest at the high-leverage nodes, where a small share already
buys real power. One statistic, \textbf{cross-unit correlation}, catches the
coordination every version of this attack requires (benign fleets diversify;
a compromised one moves in lockstep).
\end{means}

% =====================================================================
\act{Act V}{The follow-up: once they're in, they can wreck the hardware}

Market-making is the primary weapon; hardware damage is the follow-up. A controller
that can coordinate dispatch can also abuse it. Two concrete vectors, both bounded
and both loud in telemetry:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Inverter switching.} Flipping each unit between charge and discharge
as fast as possible burns power-electronics life (each flip is one thermal cycle).
Constant $1$~Hz switching destroys an inverter in \textbf{${\sim}6$ days}. A
minimum-dwell rule alone is \emph{not} enough (5-minute dwell still allows $288$
flips/day); a hard \textbf{switching-count cap of $\le 48$/day} restores ${\sim}30$-year
life.
\item \textbf{Slow, hidden wear and cost erosion.} A patient controller nudges
dispatch a little every day --- accelerating battery aging up to ${\sim}7\times$, or
bleeding cost invisibly. Undetected over six months that is \textbf{\$18--117M}. But
because a stealthier attack does less per day yet takes proportionally longer to
catch, a \textbf{cumulative (CUSUM) trend monitor} bounds total damage to
\textbf{${\sim}\$7$M} no matter how slow the attacker goes.
\end{itemize}

\begin{means}
\textbf{Firmware guardrails} (switching-count cap, minimum dwell, ramp limit,
C-rate ceiling, state-of-charge floor) plus a \textbf{cumulative trend monitor}. An
abnormal switching rate or a persistent one-way bias is glaringly obvious in
telemetry.
\end{means}

% =====================================================================
\qsec{What ties it all together --- and what Base should do}

Every attack above needs the \textbf{same ingredient: many units doing the same
thing at the same time}. Normal batteries are diverse; their actions cancel. So one
cheap statistic --- \emph{how alike the units are behaving} --- flags coordinated
manipulation whatever its goal, and the controls layer cleanly:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{No withholding or charging under low reserves.} During scarcity the
fleet supports the grid, never withdraws or holds back. \emph{(Removes the physical
crash path.)}
\item \textbf{Per-node market-share screen, weighted by price leverage $a_n$.} Cap
how large a coordinated share the fleet may hold on any high-leverage node during
scarcity. \emph{(Removes the market-power path.)}
\item \textbf{Watch coordination.} Alarm when units behave too alike (cross-unit
correlation $>0.2$) plus a slow-trend (CUSUM) monitor. \emph{(Catches price,
degradation, and stealth attacks at once.)}
\item \textbf{Per-feeder penetration caps} (set per neighborhood type) and a
synchronized-dispatch interlock. \emph{(Removes local overloads.)}
\item \textbf{Firmware guardrails.} Switching-count cap ($\le 48$/day), min dwell,
ramp and C-rate limits, SoC floor. \emph{(Protects the hardware.)}
\end{enumerate}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=baseblue!7,colframe=baseblue,boxrule=0.7pt,arc=2pt,
  title=Bottom line,fonttitle=\bfseries,coltitle=white]
The same coordination that makes a distributed fleet valuable is an attack surface.
Its ceiling is \textbf{low today} --- $0.4\%$ of the grid, no cold-start crash ---
but it \textbf{grows with scale} (a two-year clock to the crash threshold), is
\textbf{already negative-sum} in the market (\$1 earned per \$61 imposed), and is
\textbf{shaped by topology} (leverage concentrates on single-gateway grids). Every
one of these is bounded, cheaply detectable, and closed by the five controls above.
The math that prices the fleet's value also prices its danger --- so the fix ships
with the product.
\end{tcolorbox}
